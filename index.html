Where the snow numbers come from. For about the next week, the National Weather Service forecast, which meteorologists build from a blend of models on a 2.5 km grid that sees the real terrain. After that, the average of 51 runs of the European (ECMWF) ensemble, drawn as lighter bars because they're spread out by timing disagreements: a day showing 2" might be a dry day or the day a foot arrives. The "up to" figure is what 1 run in 10 exceeds. Tile totals show the middle half of the runs' totals as the likely range.

How to read it. First chair is what falls between 4 PM the evening before and 9 AM; it's blank on outlook days, which have no hourly timing. Sky and gusts cover lift hours, 9 AM to 4 PM. Shaded columns are days 8 to 15, where forecast skill drops off; treat them as a trend, not a plan.

Chains. A forecast of snow on the highest pass along each route, not actual Caltrans controls. "Likely" means at least half an inch during the drive up (5–11 AM) or home (2–8 PM), or an inch overnight before the drive up, with temperatures cold enough to stick. "Possible" means lighter snow in those windows. Hover a cell to see which pass and window set it off. For what's posted right now, check Caltrans QuickMap.

What this isn't. There's no resort-specific correction like OpenSnow's, so totals can still run low on the crest in big storms. Model snow uses a temperature-based snow ratio (8:1 near freezing up to 16:1 in cold air); the NWS applies its own. Temperature, sky and wind come from a single model blend, and model cloud cover counts thin high cloud, so "overcast" can still feel bright.
